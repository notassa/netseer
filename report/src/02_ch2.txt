@chapter CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW
@h2 2.1 Introduction
In small network environments such as small offices and home offices (SOHO), small businesses and educational laboratories, intrusion detection tools may produce alert output that is highly technical. This is especially difficult for users who do not have strong cybersecurity knowledge. It creates a gap between detection and understanding, in which a system may successfully generate alerts while users still struggle to understand what the alerts mean or what actions should be taken. A non-expert administrator may see an alert name, an IP address and a severity value without understanding whether the alert indicates reconnaissance, an exploit attempt or a serious security incident. For this reason, post-detection alert interpretation is an important area of study.
This chapter reviews previous studies and concepts related to intrusion detection and alert interpretation. Section 2.2 discusses intrusion detection systems, Suricata as the detection source, the structure of intrusion detection alerts, and the relationship between alerts, events and incidents. Section 2.3 reviews alert post-processing approaches, including normalisation, contextual enrichment, aggregation and correlation, and risk-based prioritisation, leading to the definition of Alert-Context Processing (ACP). Section 2.4 reviews AI-assisted alert interpretation, explainability in intrusion detection contexts, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and interpretation validation. Section 2.5 discusses web-based alert analysis systems. Sections 2.6 and 2.7 summarise and compare the limitations of previous studies. Section 2.8 discusses datasets used in previous studies, with a focus on UNSW-NB15. Section 2.9 compares existing systems with the proposed system, Section 2.10 presents the overall research gap and positioning, and Section 2.11 summarises the chapter.
@h2 2.2 Intrusion Detection and Alert Interpretation
@h3 2.2.1 Intrusion Detection Systems
An Intrusion Detection System (IDS) is an important defence layer within a network environment. As described by Diana et al. (2025), intrusion detection systems monitor activity on the network or host to detect indications of malicious behaviour or policy violations.
Intrusion detection systems are commonly categorised by where they are deployed and how they detect. A Network-based Intrusion Detection System (NIDS) observes traffic on a network segment, while a Host-based Intrusion Detection System (HIDS) monitors activity on a specific endpoint (Diana et al., 2025). NIDS tools are useful for monitoring small networks because they can observe traffic between devices and external networks without installing software agents on each endpoint.
The common detection approaches are signature-based, anomaly-based and hybrid detection. Abdulganiyu et al. (2023) review these approaches and note that signature-based detection matches traffic against preset rules, making it effective for known attacks, while many existing reviews place greater emphasis on anomaly-based and deep-learning models. Signature-based detection cannot detect new or unknown attacks that are outside its rule base (Hdidou & El Alami, 2024). Anomaly-based detection can detect abnormal behaviour but may produce more false alarms. Diana et al. (2025) also point out that reducing false positives and false negatives and detecting zero-day attacks remain open challenges. Machine-learning and deep-learning approaches are increasingly studied, but they mainly focus on improving detection performance rather than on post-detection interpretation.

@h3 2.2.2 Suricata as the Detection Source
Suricata is an open-source network security monitoring engine that can operate as an intrusion detection system, an Intrusion Prevention System (IPS) and a network security monitoring tool. Suricata is a rule-based engine with multi-threading capabilities to handle high-speed traffic (Ghazi et al., 2024), in contrast to older single-threaded engines. Waleed et al. (2022) evaluate Suricata against other open-source intrusion detection and prevention systems for typical Small and Medium-sized Enterprise (SME) deployments, taking into account resource consumption, throughput and packet-drop behaviour. Suricata is used as the detection source in the proposed system because it is open source, widely used, and able to analyse traffic either live or offline from packet-capture (PCAP) files, which suits both offline analysis of PCAP files and real-time monitoring of a small network.
Suricata produces structured event records that can be parsed by external systems. Tahir et al. (2024) propose a Suricata-based deployment that detects denial-of-service activity and enables prevention actions. Mattila (2020) shows how external threat-intelligence data can be transformed into Suricata rules to increase detection coverage. Suricata alert records contain fields such as timestamp, signature identifier, signature name, category, severity, source and destination addresses, ports, protocol and flow information (Open Information Security Foundation, 2024). These fields provide the basic evidence required for post-detection processing: timestamps support temporal grouping, addresses support correlation, and signature identifiers can be used to retrieve rule-related knowledge. In addition to alerts, Suricata can log protocol metadata such as DNS, HTTP, TLS, DHCP and flow records in the same eve.json file, which can be used as passive context.
Although Suricata output is structured, it still needs to be parsed and interpreted. Raharjo and Salman (2023) report that Suricata detection accuracy may decrease when the number of active rules becomes very large, so the volume and quality of alerts depend on configuration. A user may see a signature name or category without knowing what the alert means, how serious it is, or how it relates to other alerts. Hence, Suricata is treated as the detection component and the proposed system as a post-detection interpretation layer.

@h3 2.2.3 IDS Alert Structure and Technical Interpretability
Intrusion detection alerts typically contain technical evidence such as a timestamp, rule identifier, alert name, classification, priority, source and destination addresses, ports, protocol and flow information. These fields help an analyst determine which rule was triggered and where the suspicious traffic occurred. However, the presence of technical fields does not guarantee that an alert will be easily understood by non-technical users. Bhusal and Rastogi (2022) note that security monitoring tools generate alerts that lack detail about why and in what context the alert was triggered, which can make it challenging even for trained analysts to understand what an alert means.
This difference can be described as the difference between detection traceability and operational interpretability. Detection traceability is the ability to trace an alert back to the rule or condition that caused it, which is typically possible in a rule-based engine such as Suricata because each alert is associated with a signature. Operational interpretability is the ability of the user to interpret the alert in the context of their own network. According to Albasheer et al. (2022), NIDS produce large numbers of low-quality and false alerts, which makes interpretation time-consuming. If the user does not understand the signature name, the attack category, the affected service or the potential impact, an alert may be difficult to interpret even if it is traceable.
Furthermore, an intrusion detection alert does not necessarily mean that an intrusion was successful. An alert can represent suspicious traffic, an attempted exploit, reconnaissance, malformed traffic or a policy violation. Sikos (2020) points out that packet-level evidence must be analysed and interpreted before it becomes meaningful for investigation. If an alert is not correlated or placed in context, the user may not know whether it is a one-off or a recurring occurrence, or whether it relates to other activity. Alert interpretation therefore has to explain what was observed, why the alert was produced, what it may mean, and what it does not mean.

@h3 2.2.4 Alert, Event and Incident
The terms alert, event and incident are closely connected but distinct in security monitoring. An event is an observable activity within a network or system, such as a connection attempt, a domain name query, an HTTP request or an exchange of packets. Bhusal and Rastogi (2022) describe how monitoring tools consume event logs and generate threat alerts that are then reviewed by security analysts. An alert is therefore a notification generated when an event, or a group of events, is identified as potentially malicious. An incident is a higher-level security concern that may comprise multiple related alerts and events.
Intrusion detection systems may raise multiple alerts for a single activity. Kotenko et al. (2022) explain that correlating events reduces redundant data and allows analysts to identify multi-step attacks. Albasheer et al. (2022) describe how correlating similar alerts provides a better understanding of the overall course of an attack and can give insight into attacker intentions. Without correlation, a NIDS produces numerous alerts for a single activity and leaves the administrator to piece together the components of an attack. For example, a port scan followed by service enumeration and then an exploit attempt would more usefully be presented as one possible incident rather than as separate alerts.
The proposed system uses these definitions by converting raw Suricata records into normalised alerts, adding context about the related events, and grouping related alerts into possible incidents. This allows the dashboard to present each alert as part of a larger security issue rather than as an isolated problem.

@h2 2.3 IDS Alert Post-Processing and Interpretation Approaches
@h3 2.3.1 Alert Normalisation
Alert normalisation converts raw alert records into a standardised format that subsequent processing modules can rely on. Because different IDS engines use different field names, data formats and structures, normalisation is valuable even when only one engine is used, since it maps all required fields into a uniform data model. Kotenko et al. (2022) identify data normalisation as the first step in correlating security events: it creates a common representation of the data and removes redundancies before related events are grouped or associated. In the proposed method, Suricata records are normalised before enrichment, aggregation, correlation and prioritisation.
Prior research on IDS alert post-processing recognises normalisation as a precursor to aggregation and correlation, because alerts must share comparable fields before they can be grouped or linked. Albasheer et al. (2022) describe alert pre-processing as preparing NIDS alerts for similarity-based or statistical correlation by standardising them. Kotenko et al. (2022) also state that normalising events reduces the overall amount of data and facilitates comparison with other events. In practice, timestamps need to be converted into a single format, IP addresses and hosts need to be represented consistently, and missing data needs to be handled safely.
Normalisation also supports evidence traceability. The system preserves the original record of each alert and creates a normalised version for processing, which allows later explanations to refer back to the original evidence. Albasheer et al. (2022) emphasise that the quality of correlation depends on the quality of the pre-processed input, which reinforces the need for careful and lossless normalisation.

@h3 2.3.2 Contextual Enrichment
Contextual enrichment adds information to an alert to support understanding. Without enrichment, an alert may simply report communication between two addresses; with enrichment, it can also state whether the source is internal or external, whether the destination is a server, or whether the source has a poor reputation. Mattila (2020) shows how external threat-intelligence data can enhance detection by linking traffic to known malicious indicators, and Liu et al. (2022) present a context-aware method that uses provenance and context to make alert investigation more efficient.
Several sources of context can be used. Network context includes internal or external direction, subnet classification, protocols and services. Device context includes host name, operating system and vendor. Application context includes DNS queries, HTTP host names and TLS Server Name Indication (SNI). Sikos (2020) demonstrates that packet analysis can be used to obtain host, protocol and service evidence from captured traffic. In addition, the CVSS v4.0 specification (FIRST, 2023) allows severity to be refined according to environmental and asset context.
Contextual enrichment also has limitations. Passive enrichment depends on the content of the captured traffic: if there is no DHCP, DNS, ARP or fingerprintable traffic in the capture, the system may not be able to determine the host name or operating system. Information that cannot be determined must be recorded as unknown. The proposed system does not invent host names, operating systems or asset roles, and does not infer attack success without evidence. This is particularly important with datasets such as UNSW-NB15, in which host identities and operating systems are not always available or reliable.

@h3 2.3.3 Alert Aggregation and Correlation
Alert aggregation and alert correlation are post-processing techniques that reduce alert overload and improve understanding of intrusion detection output. The two techniques are related but serve different purposes. Aggregation groups repeated or very similar alerts into fewer groups, while correlation finds relationships between different alerts that may represent a wider incident.
Aggregation is commonly used when an engine produces many repeated alerts for the same activity. Kotenko et al. (2022) describe aggregation as reducing the volume of the original data stream by combining events and removing redundancy. Aggregation allows multiple alerts to be represented as a single group with a first-seen time, last-seen time, total count and representative evidence. However, aggregation should not be confused with false-positive reduction: it reduces the number of items shown but does not prove that the grouped alerts are false positives. The proposed system therefore describes aggregation as a reduction in alert volume rather than false-positive removal, unless alerts are verified against ground truth.
Alert correlation goes beyond aggregation by establishing relationships between different alerts. Albasheer et al. (2022) describe correlation as linking alerts by similarity and causality to produce a higher-level view of attempted intrusions, and classify correlation approaches into similarity-based, statistical-based, knowledge-based and hybrid approaches. Kotenko et al. (2022) similarly show that event correlation can detect multi-step or targeted attacks by linking alerts that belong to the same scenario. Correlation matters because a single alert may carry little evidence, while several related alerts may indicate a larger security issue. However, correlation can also introduce errors when unrelated alerts are linked. Correlated alerts should therefore be presented as possible incidents rather than confirmed attacks.

@h3 2.3.4 Risk-Based Alert Prioritisation
Risk-based alert prioritisation identifies which alerts or incidents need more attention. Many tools use rule severity as a basic indicator of importance, but severity alone is not the same as risk. Risk is commonly expressed as a function of the likelihood that a threat event occurs and the impact if it does (NIST, 2012). The Common Vulnerability Scoring System specification (FIRST, 2023) embodies a similar principle by computing a severity score from exploitability and impact metrics that can be refined using threat and environmental context. For alerts, confidence, frequency and correlation strength may influence likelihood, while severity, asset criticality and the targeted service may influence impact.
The OWASP Risk Rating Methodology (OWASP Foundation, n.d.) offers a structured way to estimate these two components. Likelihood is estimated from threat-agent and vulnerability factors and impact from technical and business factors, each rated from 0 to 9. The averages are converted into low, medium or high levels, and a three-by-three matrix combines the two levels into an overall severity from note to critical. Munir (2014) applied this methodology to rate attacks against an enterprise network and showed that it produces repeatable ratings, although the factors were selected manually by an analyst. Munir (2014) also proposed quantifying network security risk as an absolute value: the probability of attack on each node was combined into department-level and network-level risk using the inclusion-exclusion principle of probability, instead of reporting only qualitative labels. A further metric in the same work expressed the security level of a network as the share of IDS alerts in each attack class; however, such a share describes the distribution of alerts rather than risk, because adding alerts of one class lowers the share of every other class regardless of severity. Munir's work predates the five-year window adopted for this review, so it is used here as methodological precedent alongside current standards.
Several methods for prioritising intrusion detection alerts have been proposed in recent studies, many of which also highlight human-AI teaming to reduce alert fatigue. Jalalvand et al. (2024) systematically survey prioritisation criteria and methods in security operations centres, noting that analysts must focus on the most important alerts because investigation time is limited. Gelman et al. (2023) demonstrate that predicting the actionability of alerts and incidents can reduce analyst workload. However, if users cannot see how a score was generated, it is difficult to trust it. The proposed system therefore combines these approaches: OWASP-style likelihood and impact factors are derived automatically from alert evidence and combined through the severity matrix, device and network values are aggregated probabilistically as proposed by Munir (2014), and the factor breakdown is kept so that the interpretation layer can explain why an incident was classified as note, low, medium, high or critical risk.

@h3 2.3.5 Alert-Context Processing
The approaches discussed in the previous subsections, namely normalisation, enrichment, aggregation, correlation and prioritisation, are typically studied as independent techniques. The proposed system integrates them into a single deterministic approach called Alert-Context Processing (ACP). ACP is a deterministic, multi-stage intrusion detection alert post-processing technique that normalises, enriches, aggregates, correlates and prioritises Suricata alerts before constructing a structured evidence package for AI-assisted interpretation. ACP is not an intrusion detection system, a machine-learning model or a Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) system; it is a processing method applied after Suricata generates alerts.
ACP is informed by the research reviewed above. It prepares structured and traceable evidence before the AI interpretation layer is used, so that the AI is not responsible for detection or scoring but instead explains evidence that ACP has already processed and organised. Normalisation provides consistent input (Kotenko et al., 2022; Albasheer et al., 2022), enrichment adds meaning (Mattila, 2020; Liu et al., 2022), aggregation reduces volume (Kotenko et al., 2022), correlation creates possible incidents (Albasheer et al., 2022; Kotenko et al., 2022), and prioritisation orders incidents by risk (NIST, 2012; OWASP Foundation, n.d.; Jalalvand et al., 2024).
The ACP flow has seven stages. First, normalisation converts Suricata records into a common schema. Second, preliminary enrichment adds available network, device, application and threat context. Third, aggregation combines repeated alerts. Fourth, correlation connects related groups into possible incidents. Fifth, incident-level enrichment adds context at the incident level. Sixth, risk prioritisation produces a traceable risk score. Seventh, evidence-package construction prepares structured evidence for RAG and AI-assisted interpretation.

@h2 2.4 AI-Assisted IDS Alert Interpretation
@h3 2.4.1 Human-Oriented Alert Explanation
The goal of a human-oriented alert explanation is to make clear to users what an alert means and what they should do about it. Intrusion detection alerts are usually written for analysts, so an explanation should not simply repeat the alert name. Instead, it should describe what was observed, why the alert fired, how related alerts connect, why the incident is important, what evidence is missing and what investigation steps should follow. Bhusal and Rastogi (2022) highlight that raw alerts seldom explain why an alert was raised or the context in which it was raised, which analysts need.
Previous research shows that artificial intelligence can help with this task. Ban et al. (2021) propose AI-based screening tools and visualisations that help operators distinguish real threats from false alarms and act more quickly. Jalalvand et al. (2024) argue that combining human expertise with AI improves the interpretation and re-prioritisation of alerts. Likewise, Sebastian et al. (2026) report improved analyst response time when explainable, actionable insights are provided. Explanations for small-network users should avoid excessive jargon and should clearly distinguish evidence from possible interpretation; for example, an explanation should not assert that an exploit succeeded unless there is evidence to support it.
In the proposed system, the AI layer is used only after ACP has prepared the structured evidence package. The AI does not detect attacks, confirm compromise, assign the risk score or invent missing context. It translates ACP evidence into comprehensible sections, such as a summary, a technical interpretation, the evidence used, the rationale for the risk level, the uncertainty and recommended investigation steps.

@h3 2.4.2 Explainability in IDS Contexts
Explainability can refer to different problems in the context of intrusion detection. Traditional Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) usually focuses on explaining the reasoning behind a machine-learning or deep-learning model's prediction. Bhusal and Rastogi (2022) note that black-box models lack intuitive explanations of their predictions and propose metrics to evaluate explanations in security monitoring. Sebastian et al. (2026) apply post-hoc explainability to a deep-learning detector to increase transparency without accuracy loss. Similarly, Kaur et al. (2023) suggest that AI can speed up detection and response, but that its outputs should be interpretable and trustworthy.
The proposed system, however, does not explain a black-box model, because Suricata is rule-based and already provides rule traceability. As noted by Abdulganiyu et al. (2023), signature- and rule-based detection is transparent and auditable compared with opaque models. The proposed system therefore addresses post-detection interpretability, that is, explaining what rule-based evidence means in context. A Suricata alert may be tied to a rule, but users may still need to understand what the rule means, how it relates to other alerts, why it was prioritised and what the limitations of the evidence are. Explainability in the proposed system relies on evidence traceability, contextualisation, completeness, uncertainty disclosure and actionable guidance. It is thus a user-facing interpretation problem rather than a model-transparency problem.

@h3 2.4.3 Retrieval-Augmented Generation
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is a technique in which a language model retrieves relevant knowledge from an external source before generating an answer. RAG is helpful in cybersecurity because it grounds the explanation in approved knowledge such as rule descriptions, attack-technique references, protocol descriptions and investigation guidance. This is important because directly prompting a language model can lead to plausible but unsupported statements (Alhuzali, 2025).
The use of external knowledge to support security analysis is consistent with previous work. Mattila (2020) uses external threat-intelligence knowledge in a detection workflow, and Kaur et al. (2023) review how AI can automate and support cybersecurity tasks when it is supplied with appropriate information. In intrusion detection alert interpretation, retrieval can be keyed on the Suricata signature identifier, alert category, protocol or a mapped MITRE ATT&CK technique (MITRE Corporation, 2025), and the retrieved knowledge can be combined with the ACP evidence package. In the proposed system RAG does not replace ACP: ACP prepares structured incident evidence and RAG provides additional knowledge, so that the explanation is based on both the observed alert evidence and retrieved security knowledge.

@h3 2.4.4 Interpretation Validation
Because AI-generated explanations can contain plausible but unsupported statements, the interpretation produced by such a system must itself be validated. Validation in previous studies takes several complementary forms. At the detection level, IDS performance is evaluated against benchmark datasets with reliable ground truth using measures such as true-positive and false-positive rates (Diana et al., 2025); this characterises the quality of the evidence that is later explained. At the explanation level, Bhusal and Rastogi (2022) propose quantitative criteria such as fidelity and stability for explanations in security monitoring, while Es et al. (2024) propose reference-free metrics for retrieval-augmented generation, including faithfulness, which measures the proportion of generated claims that can be inferred from the retrieved context. At the human level, Sebastian et al. (2026) assess whether explanations improve analyst understanding and response time.
Hallucination is a central risk for language-model explanations in security contexts. Alhuzali (2025) shows that grounding generation in retrieved, structured threat knowledge reduces unsupported statements, but grounding alone does not guarantee that every generated statement is supported. A practical validation approach therefore combines automatic checks with human assessment: generated statements are traced back to the evidence they rely on, the proportion of unsupported claims is measured, and representative users judge whether the explanation is understandable, useful and honest about uncertainty.
The proposed system adopts these criteria in two ways. First, at run time, every explanation is checked automatically against the ACP evidence package: each claim must cite evidence items, technical entities such as IP addresses, ports, signature identifiers and ATT&CK technique identifiers must exist in the evidence, the stated risk level must match the ACP risk band, and assertions of compromise are flagged unless supported. Second, during evaluation, faithfulness, unsupported-claim rate, factual accuracy, coverage, clarity of uncertainty and usefulness of recommended actions are measured, together with user comprehension.

@h2 2.8 Datasets Used in Previous Studies
@h3 2.8.1 Common IDS Datasets
Datasets are important in intrusion detection research because they provide traffic samples, attack labels and test scenarios for evaluation. Recent reviews of intrusion detection and event correlation, such as Abdulganiyu et al. (2023), Diana et al. (2025) and Kotenko et al. (2022), discuss a range of benchmark datasets used to evaluate detection and correlation, including older scenario datasets and newer labelled datasets. These datasets vary in age, realism and labelling quality, which affects how trustworthy they are for evaluation. The dataset chosen for evaluation should therefore provide realistic traffic as well as reliable ground-truth labels.
@h3 2.8.2 UNSW-NB15
UNSW-NB15 is a network intrusion dataset that contains labelled normal and attack traffic across several attack categories and is commonly used in intrusion detection research (Moustafa & Slay, 2015). UNSW-NB15 is useful for the proposed system because its PCAP files can be processed by Suricata to generate alerts, which allows the post-detection pipeline to be tested with real packet data rather than manually created alert samples. UNSW-NB15 provides ground-truth labels for the traffic, but this does not mean that Suricata will detect all malicious traffic. The alerts generated from UNSW-NB15 must therefore be validated against the ground truth.
@h3 2.8.3 Dataset Validation Requirements
Because intrusion detection evaluation depends on dataset quality, validation requirements must be stated clearly. As Diana et al. (2025) observe, evaluating detection performance requires benchmark datasets with reliable ground truth and measures such as true-positive and false-positive rates. In the proposed system, Suricata alerts generated from UNSW-NB15 are validated against the dataset ground truth using attributes such as timestamp, source and destination addresses, ports, protocol and attack category where available, and each alert is classified as a true positive, a false positive or uncertain. Where host identity, operating system, DHCP, ARP or passive fingerprinting information is unavailable, this is treated as a dataset limitation and the corresponding context is marked as unknown rather than fabricated.

@h2 2.10 Overall Research Gap and Positioning
The literature shows that intrusion detection, alert aggregation, alert correlation, risk prioritisation, explainability and AI-assisted explanation have all been studied, but often in isolation or as part of enterprise-oriented tools. Detection research provides methods for identifying suspicious traffic (Abdulganiyu et al., 2023; Diana et al., 2025), post-processing research provides methods for grouping and correlating alerts (Kotenko et al., 2022; Albasheer et al., 2022), prioritisation research orders alerts by risk (Jalalvand et al., 2024; Gelman et al., 2023), and explainability research examines how analysts can understand alerts (Bhusal & Rastogi, 2022).
However, these capabilities are not generally incorporated into a lightweight Suricata-based interpretation system for small-network users. Many dashboards still focus on displaying technical alert fields, correlation and prioritisation methods are built for analysts or complex environments, and AI-assisted explanation can generate unsupported statements if it is not based on structured evidence. In small-network environments a practical and understandable approach is needed, because users may be able to deploy open-source tools such as Suricata but lack the expertise to interpret raw alerts, correlate related activity or judge risk.
The proposed system is framed as a post-detection interpretation layer. It does not replace Suricata, does not improve detection accuracy, does not detect zero-day attacks and does not function as a full SIEM. Its contribution is to translate Suricata alert evidence into contextualised, risk-prioritised and evidence-based explanations for small-network users through Alert-Context Processing and verified AI-assisted interpretation. In summary, the research gap is as follows: existing research offers mature methods for detection, correlation, enrichment, prioritisation and AI-assisted explanation, but these methods are rarely combined into a lightweight interpretation system for small-network users.
@h2 2.11 Chapter Summary
This chapter reviewed previous studies and concepts on intrusion detection, Suricata alert output, alert interpretability, alert post-processing, contextual enrichment, aggregation, correlation, risk-based prioritisation, AI-assisted explanation, RAG, interpretation validation, web-based alert analysis systems and datasets. The review shows that intrusion detection tools provide valuable detection evidence, but raw alerts can still be difficult to interpret without additional context.
The chapter also described the difference between detection traceability and operational interpretability. While Suricata alerts are rule-based and traceable, they can still be difficult for non-expert users to interpret, which supports the need for a post-detection interpretation layer that transforms raw alert evidence into more meaningful information. Prior work supports individual components such as normalisation, aggregation, correlation, risk prioritisation and grounded explanation, but these components are often studied in isolation or implemented in systems for trained analysts and enterprise environments. The proposed system bridges this gap by integrating these ideas through ACP and verified AI-assisted interpretation for small-network users.
